AI Governance in Fintech: Five Practical Questions

Following Web Summit Lisbon 2026, one theme deserves particular attention: how do fintech teams turn interest in artificial intelligence into systems that customers, partners and regulators can trust?

By Igor Marchetti Lippi · Lippi

The conversation around AI often begins with new capabilities. For fintech businesses, the harder work comes next: defining who is accountable, what a system may do, how it is monitored and what happens when it gets something wrong.

These are five practical questions founders and leadership teams can use to frame their next discussion. They are reflections on the issues relevant to fintech, not quotations or findings attributed to specific Web Summit speakers.

1. What decision is the AI system making?

Start with the intended use, not the model. Is AI assisting an employee, recommending an action or acting independently? The answer affects approval limits, escalation paths, customer disclosures and the evidence a team should retain.

2. Who remains accountable?

An automated process does not eliminate management responsibility. Identify the business owner, risk owner and decision-maker who can pause or override the system, particularly where customer outcomes or payments are involved.

3. Can we explain the data and controls?

Map the data being used, its permitted purpose, access arrangements and quality checks. Documentation, testing and clear records matter as much as performance metrics, especially when personal or sensitive information may be involved.

4. What changes when an AI agent acts?

Agentic workflows can introduce a new operational question: what is an agent authorised to do on somebody's behalf? Payment limits, consent, authentication, exceptions and audit trails should be considered before allowing autonomous actions.

5. How will we know if something goes wrong?

Useful governance is ongoing. Define monitoring indicators, incident routes, review frequency and third-party dependencies. Establish what triggers human review, remediation or a suspension of the AI-enabled service.

From principles to practical implementation

Fintech teams do not necessarily need a larger policy library to begin. A short use-case inventory, named accountable owners, risk-based testing, measurable controls and a documented escalation process provide a more useful starting point than generic statements of responsible AI.

In Europe, relevant obligations depend on the actual use case and business model. Firms should assess applicable requirements, including data protection, financial-sector rules and the EU AI Act where relevant, rather than assuming every AI system is regulated identically.

The central question is not simply “Can we use AI?” It is “Can we demonstrate that we use it responsibly?”
